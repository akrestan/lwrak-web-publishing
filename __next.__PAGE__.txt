4:T9e5,In keeping with my introductory post on LinkedIn [here](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7403529869227274240), I welcome you to this specialized miniblog. The posts are based on my direct personal experiences, collected from my participation in 10+ projects, some engagements being longer, some shorter.



Yet, in hindsight, they all appear to manifest the same set of reoccurring problems; each project having its own specific dose of the discussed symptoms. Those projects' shortcomings are attributable to various things such as the lack of understanding of the Agile Manifesto's principles, lack of experience in leading the team, lack of interest in the later phases, and also neglect.
If I were to choose one that sticks out: Agile Manifesto's principles are not read as lessons learned by others that are to be re-interpreted and re-implemented in full in the context of the SW solution that is being built. The most visibly missing ingredient is sustainability.



Despite being rooted in concrete environments the blog posts are written abstractly and are mostly centered on 3 main archetypes participating in the SW development process: the organization representative a.k.a. the boss, the development lead, and the developer.



Focusing on this last role, I hope the leaders — those managing SW development and operations — will, after reading the blog, think more about how they hire. Recruitment for SW development positions shouldn't be just about checking a candidate’s proficiency in a specific tech stack.
If for nothing else then because the tech stack may become obsolete by the time the project starts preparing a "version 3" — "version X" being a figure of speech in an environment where we should strive for the work flow to be continuous.
Along with giving some thought to the things like how they would want the day-to-day business to be run, what the communications in the team should look like, what's the cost of any kind of change (including bringing a new team member up to speed) after an MVP has been delivered and the first success celebrated.



The bottom line: SW developers are not the same kind of people as programmers. I have met great programmers (far better than me), decent programmers and poor programmers; I have also met good SW developers and bad ones.
I have met a solid programmer who made a sub-par SW developer. However, I have yet to encounter a respectable SW developer who was a bad programmer. I encourage you to do your own research on the subject.5:Tda3,An introduction calls for an analogy and analogies, of course, limp. This one is no exception; nevertheless, it is important to bring out the contrast. The analogy revolves around the differing mindsets of a passenger aircraft manufacturer and an airline operator.



Today, as users of an airline's services, we intuitively understand that making a flight from A to B a predictable undertaking requires much more than constructing a heavier-than-air contraption that can take off and land, and rolling it out of a hangar.
We somehow assume that the physics of flight and aircraft construction have been mastered by Airbus and Boeing since the days the Wright brothers took to the skies. However, behind the scenes, the airline organization carries out many complex activities, without which air travel would be difficult, if not impossible.
These activities include creating long-term flight timetables and schedules, liaising with airports, aircraft maintenance, and their airworthiness checks. They also encompass pre-flight planning — which involves route selection, fuel calculation, fueling, and weather condition examination — as well as pilot training and certification, crew shift rotation, and mandatory rest periods.
Finally, to ensure the experience is as enjoyable and safe as possible, they cover cabin crew preparations, catering, and cleaning — to name just a few.
Airline operators simply must engage in these complex organizational activities to make the basic transport function of an aircraft practically utilizable — all while trying to maintain profitability.



A software project is in a similar situation, except that — and here is where the analogy begins to falter — the airline a) buys the creation that delivers the essential functionality instead of home-building it, and b) nobody asks to reshape or change the core functionalities of the airplanes during their lifespan.
A SW solution, the project under which it is developed, and the organizational framework used for running the project, on the other hand, need to do all that at the same time. The project members need to think about testability, security, performance, source code maintainability, deployment, monitoring, site reliability, project staff rotation, and the like.
From the onset, day after day, all while the whole thing is not even flying yet from the user perspective — and all while trying to stay within the available budget.



The Agile Manifesto recognizes the fact that sustained excellence is only possible when the operations part is also involved, not just the user functionality, even though it does not explicitly state it. This is why the work style needs to be customized to accommodate the particular circumstances. It is by no means a one-size-fits-all approach and it is, by all means, hard.



It's hard to deliver a good quality SW solution when you are aware that both points of view — function and operations — are needed; it's impossible to do it if one is either unaware of the full scale, complacent, or simply chooses to ignore the facts.
Thus, I maintain that for SW projects to be successful, organizations need to develop the mindset of an airline that also needs to make its own aircraft, rather than to settle for the mindset of an aircraft manufacturer. Of all the gigs I took part in that claimed to be agile, all except one failed to a lesser or greater degree on this point. Where and how exactly is the subject of the remaining chapters.6:T10e5,This mentality that goes with "we'll spare no expense to cut the (IT) costs" is embedded in the budget-driven corporations' DNA. On one hand, I do understand it: everything has to make some economic sense and corporate pockets are not bottomless;
on the other hand, the cost cutting exercises that take place every year in a corporation are mechanical and not focused on squeezing out the maximum value from the available resources. Most importantly, the cost cuts are someone's easily measurable personal KPIs whereas the consequences are not (as in, "we hired two cost-control managers but fired the cleaner to offset the extra expenses").
In the field of financing the corporate IT, this may, over time, have become a less blatant and pressing issue as the general public knowledge of how SW needs to be verified, staged and readied for production has changed since the 2000s to favour predictability — or rather to avoid the cost associated with unpredictability and failures — but the attitude stays inherently present.
So, let's focus on some of the symptoms even though they might be less obvious or visible today than before.



A long time ago —  but still in this century — I remember how my team had to haggle over a server-like machine to run a CI/CD FOSS suite for nightly builds, a community knowledge-sharing forum application, and a bug tracking tool. The box was to be stuck underneath someone's desk, without support and maintenance, and not to be publicly spoken of. There was an issue with financing the license for the bug tracking tool too, if my memory serves.
Put simply, to operate a CI/CD pipeline in that place meant to engage in an intra-company guerrilla warfare, people's efficiency did not matter a single bit. By the same token, in some other place you wouldn't get a 27" monitor, but you could get two 24" as they came cheaper. Or the Slack chat application for developers would be discontinued on the grounds that "we have now purchased Teams for everyone!".
Or a paid productivity plugin for an IDE worth 30 bucks per developer a year is too much, but a wastefully run development team that forces developers to manually repeat automatable tasks is not.
It is by no means just about expenditures on hardware and SW licenses. All of the above also applies to the developers' education, training and experience building in this fast changing industry.
I ended my employment with one company at the point when — after a lot of time spent on explaining the differences between an office notebook and a developer notebook, and that _IT IS_ a waste of money if the developers have to wait 20+ minutes for a local Maven build to complete — my boss proposed that I buy and bring over my own notebook.
Back then it would have been an equivalent of my two months' net pay, no corporate bulk order discount.



If I may bring to your attention one Web site that dates back even before the IT project planning style borrowed from the construction industry was labelled Waterfall and the first attempts at an agile development were better known as eXtreme Programming; it's Joel Spolsky's [12 Steps to Better Code]( https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/08/09/the-joel-test-12-steps-to-better-code), in particular the step #9 that reads "Do you use the best tools money can buy?"
I learned the hard way that the employers, represented by "the bosses", in general fail on this point, i.e. to provide adequate equipment and education, and it's a waste of time trying to explain or persuade them. Instead, I went freelancing with my own properly sized notebook and an operating system of my choice, my own monitor and keyboard, and SW licences I pay for myself so that they allow me to be efficient.



There is one particular type of glaring and pitiful economic nonsense within corporations that I repeatedly witnessed in several places that is in stark contrast to the claimed savings or cost optimizations carried out by the organization and that is the management of the knowledge gained by the developers while on the project;
or, to put it another way, the cost that has to be paid by the organization when a skilled, business-familiar developer with project-specific experience is let go and a new one must be hired.
